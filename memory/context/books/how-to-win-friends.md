# How to Win Friends and Influence People
**Author:** Dale Carnegie | **Processed:** 2026-03-15

---

## Core Thesis
People are fundamentally driven by the desire to feel important and valued. Genuine influence comes not from manipulation or argument, but from sincere interest in others — making them feel seen, heard, and respected. You cannot force people to change; you can only create the conditions where they want to.

---

## Key Mental Models

### 1. The Three Don'ts
Never criticize, condemn, or complain. These actions create defensiveness and resentment — the exact opposite of what you need when trying to move someone. People don't respond to being wrong; they respond to feeling understood.

### 2. Honest, Specific Appreciation
Flattery is cheap and recognized. Honest appreciation — tied to a specific action or quality — is rare and remembered. The difference: "You're great" vs "The way you handled that seller call when they went sideways — that was calm and sharp."

### 3. Arouse Eager Want
The only way to get someone to do something is to make them want to do it. Don't think about what you want. Think about what they want — then show them how your ask serves their interest.

### 4. The Listening Advantage
Most people listen to reply. The rare person who listens to *understand* commands enormous trust and influence. Ask questions, let the other person do most of the talking, and never interrupt.

### 5. Make Them Feel Important
Every person you talk to has a need to feel significant. Feed that need genuinely — with their name, specific acknowledgment of their value, and real curiosity about their situation — and they will move mountains for you.

## Application to NAH (New Again Houses)

**Seller Conversations:**
The moment a seller feels like a mark instead of a person, the deal is dead. Kyle, Daniel, and Chris should be trained to lead with curiosity, not pitch. Ask about their situation. Listen twice as long as you talk. The seller who feels heard is the seller who signs.

**Team Management:**
Criticizing Kyle or Daniel in a way that makes them feel stupid closes them off. Specific appreciation for what they're doing right — even while correcting — keeps them coachable. "You closed that call strong. Here's the one thing to tighten..." works. "You messed that up" doesn't.

**Buyer/Dispo Relationships:**
Esteban's buyer relationships are long-game assets. Buyers who feel remembered (name, past deals, their preferences) come back. Generic treatment produces generic loyalty.

## Application to Gunner

**User Onboarding:**
Users who feel like Gunner "gets" them stay. Onboarding should feel like Gunner is genuinely curious about their team and goals — not just collecting data. Frame questions around what *they* care about, not what the product needs.

**Sales Calls:**
The Gunner sales rep (or Corey, when he's selling it) wins by making the prospect feel understood, not impressed. Let them talk about their pain. Don't rush to the pitch.

**Team Coaching Dynamics:**
Gunner grades calls and surfaces mistakes. Framing matters enormously — the same feedback lands differently depending on whether the rep feels judged or supported. Carnegie is the operating system for how Gunner delivers feedback.

## Behavioral Rules for Xhaka

1. **When Corey reports a difficult conversation with a seller or team member**, surface Carnegie's core question first: *What did they want from that interaction? Did they feel heard?* Only then suggest tactics.

2. **When Corey is frustrated with a team member's performance**, before recommending any corrective action, ask: *Has Corey specifically acknowledged what that person is doing right recently?* Appreciation before correction.

3. **When Corey is preparing for a negotiation or tough conversation**, remind him: the person across the table has a need to feel important. What's one specific, genuine thing he can acknowledge about them before getting into the substance?

4. **When Corey is designing a script or onboarding flow for any product/team**, apply the Carnegie filter: *Does this make the other person feel important and heard, or does it make the company feel smart?*

5. **When Corey reports a deal that went sideways**, ask whether the seller/buyer felt genuinely understood — before analyzing price or terms. Most deals die on rapport, not numbers.
