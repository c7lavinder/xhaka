# Meditations
**Author:** Marcus Aurelius | **Processed:** 2026-03-15

---

## Core Thesis
Stoic philosophy as daily practice. You cannot control what happens to you — markets, people, outcomes. You can only control your judgments, responses, and effort. Everything else is noise. The discipline is applying this not once, but in the ten thousand small moments every day when your instinct is to react, blame, or despair.

---

## Key Mental Models

### 1. The Dichotomy of Control
Divide every situation into two buckets: what is up to you (your effort, your response, your attitude, your choices) and what is not (the economy, other people's behavior, the outcome of any given deal). Suffering comes from treating the second bucket like it belongs in the first.

### 2. Memento Mori — Mortality as Clarifier
Regularly contemplating that you will die — and that your problems will largely be forgotten — is not pessimistic. It's clarifying. The trivialities that consume energy look different when viewed against the backdrop of finitude. What actually matters?

### 3. Amor Fati — Love of Fate
Not just accepting what happens, but loving it — treating obstacles as the work, not the interruption. The Stoic doesn't ask "why did this happen to me?" They ask "how does this fit into what I'm supposed to become?"

### 4. The Obstacle Is the Way
Every impediment to action advances action. What stands in the way becomes the way. A deal falling through teaches you something a closed deal never could. A team failure surfaces a process gap you needed to see.

### 5. Morning/Evening Reflection
Marcus kept this journal as a practice — not for publication, but for calibration. Review the day. What did you do well? Where did you lose your composure? What would you do differently? This is how a human being actually improves.

## Application to NAH

**Deal Losses and Market Shifts:**
The deal fell through. The market softened. A seller backed out. These are not reasons to spiral — they are inputs. What's in Corey's control: his next conversation, his team's training, his process adjustment. Not the deal outcome.

**Team Failures:**
When Kyle misses a close or Daniel runs a bad call, the stoic question is not "why did they do that" but "what is in Corey's control here?" Better process, better training, better clarity of expectations — all in the bucket.

**Composure as a Leadership Asset:**
Corey's team watches how he responds to adversity. A leader who spirals when things go wrong creates a team that hides bad news. A leader who responds with "okay, what do we control here?" creates a team that surfaces problems early.

---

## Application to Gunner

**Product Setbacks:**
A feature didn't land. A customer churned. A competitor launched something better. These are all in the "not up to us" bucket for their cause — but 100% in the "up to us" bucket for the response. Build, iterate, improve.

**Competitor Moves:**
Watching a competitor win is a test of Stoic discipline. The response is not anxiety or mimicry — it's a clarifying question: what do *we* control that makes us better for *our* customer?

**Metrics as Input, Not Identity:**
Gunner's numbers on any given day do not define the product. They inform the next iteration. The Stoic builder doesn't celebrate or despair based on a single data point.

## Behavioral Rules for Xhaka

1. **When Corey is frustrated by something outside his control** (a seller's irrationality, a market condition, a team member's attitude), stop and split the situation: *What is actually in your control here? That's where your attention goes. Everything else is noise.*

2. **When Corey is catastrophizing or spiraling**, use the Memento Mori lens gently: *In 5 years, does this problem exist? What's the version of you that looks back at this moment as a turning point, not a disaster?*

3. **When Corey hits an obstacle** (a deal falling through, a technical failure, a team breakdown), invoke "the obstacle is the way": *What does this situation make possible that wouldn't have been possible without it?*

4. **When Corey is post-crisis and reflecting**, suggest the evening review: *What went well, what didn't, what would you do differently? This is the practice — not the theory.*

5. **When Corey is about to react emotionally to something**, surface the core Stoic question: *Is this response coming from your values, or from the impulse of the moment? What would the composed version of you do here?*
