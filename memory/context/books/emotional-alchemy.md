# Emotional Alchemy
*Tara Bennett-Goleman — 2001*

---

## Core Thesis

Our emotional schemas — deep patterns formed in childhood — distort how we perceive present reality. We don't see the world as it is; we see it through the lens of old wounds. A seller who seems unreasonable, a team member who seems defensive, a negotiation that feels personal — these may all be schema activations, not accurate readings of the present moment. Mindfulness combined with schema therapy can transform these patterns rather than suppress them.

---

## Key Mental Models

### 1. Emotional Schemas
Schemas are neural patterns built from repeated early experiences — abandonment, failure, mistrust, subjugation, entitlement. Each one creates a predictable distortion lens. When a schema activates, you're perceiving the present through a past filter. The distortion feels like reality.

Core schemas and their distortions:
- **Abandonment** → reads neutral distance as rejection
- **Failure** → interprets difficulty as proof of inadequacy
- **Mistrust** → sees benign actors as threats
- **Subjugation** → reads structure as control, compliance as loss of self
- **Emotional Deprivation** → feels perpetually unseen, even when acknowledged

### 2. The Mindfulness Gap
Between trigger and reaction, there is a gap. Most people live in automatic reaction — the trigger fires and the response follows immediately, shaped entirely by the schema. Mindfulness expands the gap. In the gap lives choice. The goal is not to eliminate the trigger or the schema — it's to widen the space where agency lives.

### 3. Alchemy vs. Suppression
Suppression pushes the emotion down. It resurfaces, often harder. Alchemy transforms it — by bringing mindful attention to the emotion, tracing it to its origin, and dissolving its grip on the present. Suppression requires perpetual effort. Alchemy is a one-time investment per pattern.

### 4. Recognizing When You're In Schema vs. Reality
The hallmark of schema activation: the emotional response feels disproportionate to the situation. Something small hits like something enormous. Or a pattern keeps recurring across different people, different settings. When the same "problem" follows you everywhere, you're the common denominator — and a schema is likely the mechanism.

### 5. The Body as Early Warning System
Schemas announce themselves physically before they reach conscious thought — tightening in the chest, a flush of heat, sudden fatigue. Learning to read these somatic signals gives you a head start on catching the schema before it runs the show.

## Application to NAH

**High-stakes negotiations trigger schemas:** A seller who seems unreasonable may be running a mistrust or abandonment schema — they've been burned before, they're in a vulnerable moment, and they're projecting. Understanding this changes the entire approach. Don't match their energy. Name the fear gently. Meet the schema, not the argument.

**Corey's own reactive patterns:** When does Corey get triggered in deals? When a seller goes cold after seeming warm? When a team member seems ungrateful? Identifying those moments — and the schema underneath — is strategic self-knowledge. The goal isn't therapy; it's not being run by invisible wiring in high-stakes moments.

**Team dynamics:** Chris or Kyle becoming defensive in feedback sessions may be running a failure or subjugation schema. Understanding this reframes the coaching approach — address the safety, not just the performance.

---

## Application to Gunner

**Sales calls are emotional events:** Reps who cave on price aren't logically concluding they should — their subjugation or abandonment schema activated when the buyer pushed back. Gunner's AI coach should flag emotional capitulation patterns, not just missed objection handling.

**Schema-aware coaching language:** When a rep over-explains when challenged, that's often a failure schema running ("prove I'm competent"). When they go quiet under pressure, that may be subjugation. Coaching that addresses *why* the pattern happens lands deeper than coaching that just corrects *what* happened.

**Onboarding emotional intelligence:** Gunner could include a brief "pressure response" profile — how does this rep typically behave under challenge? — to help managers coach to the schema, not just the script.

## Behavioral Rules for Xhaka

1. **When Corey describes a frustrating interaction that seems disproportionate**, ask: is this the present situation, or is a schema being activated? "That reaction feels bigger than the event — what does this remind you of?"

2. **When a seller or team member is behaving irrationally**, consider what schema might be driving them. Don't take it personally and don't advise Corey to, either. Name the likely fear, meet it, move through it.

3. **When advising on difficult conversations**, reference the mindfulness gap — the space between trigger and response is where the choice lives. The goal is to widen that gap, not to suppress the emotion.

4. **When a pattern keeps recurring** — same deal dynamic, same team friction, same Corey reaction — flag it as likely schema-driven. Patterns that follow you across contexts are internal, not external.

5. **When coaching a team member on a recurring failure**, look past the technique to the emotional pattern. Ask: what is happening in their body in that moment? What does that moment remind them of?

6. **Never advise emotional suppression.** Pushing it down is a short-term fix that creates long-term costs. Recommend acknowledgment, curiosity, and transformation over performance of calm.
