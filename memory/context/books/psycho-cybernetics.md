# Psycho-Cybernetics
*Maxwell Maltz — 1960*

---

## Core Thesis

The brain is a goal-seeking servo-mechanism — a cybernetic system that automatically steers toward whatever target you give it. Your **self-image** is the thermostat that sets the upper limit of your performance. You cannot consistently outperform your self-image any more than a thermostat can heat a room above its setting. Change the self-image and behavior changes automatically — no willpower required.

---

## Key Mental Models

### 1. Self-Image as the Operating Thermostat
You perform in accordance with how you see yourself, not how you want to perform. The self-image is set by past experiences, failures, and the stories you tell about them. It acts as an invisible ceiling. Trying harder within the same self-image is like turning up the heat against a locked thermostat.

### 2. Creative Imagination and Mental Rehearsal
The brain's nervous system cannot distinguish between a vividly imagined experience and a real one. Athletes who rehearse mentally improve as much as those who practice physically. Visualization is practice — treated as fully real by the subconscious servo-mechanism.

### 3. The Success Mechanism vs. The Failure Mechanism
Every person has both. The Success Mechanism moves toward goals, learns from feedback, adapts, and keeps going. The Failure Mechanism is driven by fear, rigidity, self-sabotage, and negative self-talk. The key is which one you've activated through your dominant self-image.

### 4. Relaxation as the Doorway to the Subconscious
The subconscious — where the self-image lives — is accessed through relaxation, not force. Strain and anxiety lock the subconscious down. Calm, unhurried mental rehearsal is how new patterns are seeded.

### 5. Forgiveness and Clearing Old Failure Patterns
Old failures are scar tissue on the self-image. Holding onto them keeps the thermostat low. Forgiveness — of self and others — isn't moral weakness; it's maintenance. You clear the failure pattern so the servo-mechanism can aim at something new.

## Application to Gunner

**Pre-call visualization as a product feature:** Gunner currently grades calls after the fact. Maltz suggests the higher-leverage moment is *before* the call — a brief guided rehearsal. A "pre-call warmup" feature (even a 60-second prompt) could move scores more than post-call feedback alone.

**Grading self-image, not just technique:** If a rep consistently fails at the same moment (e.g., always caves on price), that's a self-image pattern, not a knowledge gap. Gunner's AI coach could flag recurring failure patterns as identity-level, not just skill-level.

**Onboarding mental model:** New reps should be asked: "What does a great sales rep look like to you? Do you see yourself as one?" Their answer tells you more than any test score.

## Behavioral Rules for Xhaka

1. **When a team member is underperforming consistently**, ask: what is their self-image telling them they're capable of? Address the identity, not just the technique. Skills coaching on a broken self-image is wasted effort.

2. **When Corey hits a ceiling he can't seem to break through**, reference Maltz — raise the thermostat, not just the effort. Ask: "What would you have to believe about yourself to operate at the next level?"

3. **Before a high-stakes negotiation or pitch**, recommend mental rehearsal. Spend 5 minutes vividly imagining the best version of how it goes. The brain treats it as real practice.

4. **When reviewing Gunner data**, look for reps with recurring failure points at the same moment in calls — that's a self-image pattern. Surface it as identity-level feedback, not technique correction.

5. **When someone says "I'm just not good at X"**, flag it as a self-image statement, not a fact. Maltz: that belief is the ceiling, not a diagnosis.

6. **When Corey is holding onto a past failure** (a deal that went wrong, a hire that didn't work), invoke the forgiveness principle — not as comfort, but as maintenance. Clear it so the mechanism can aim forward.
