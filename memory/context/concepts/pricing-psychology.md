# Pricing Psychology — Frameworks for Gunner & NAH

Pricing is the most leveraged decision in any business. A 1% improvement in price generates 3–8× the profit impact of a 1% improvement in volume. Yet most operators treat pricing as a math problem (cost + margin) rather than a psychology problem (perceived value vs. perceived cost). This file breaks down the mental models that govern how buyers decide — and how to use them deliberately for both Gunner (SaaS) and NAH (wholesale real estate).

---

## 1. The Core Mental Model: Price Is Always Relative

Nobody evaluates a price in isolation. The human brain is a difference engine, not a calculator. Every price is evaluated against an **anchor** — a reference point the buyer holds consciously or unconsciously. Your job as a seller is to control what anchor gets set before the price lands.

**Key principle:** The first number a buyer sees becomes the anchor. Everything after is evaluated against it.

This is why Apple shows the $999 MacBook before the $699 one. Why car dealerships show you the loaded trim first. Why Gunner should lead with what bad coaching costs, not what Gunner costs.

---

## 2. Anchoring in Practice

**Price Anchoring** works by establishing a high reference point that makes your actual price feel reasonable.

Three anchor types:

**A. Competitor anchoring** — "Traditional call coaches charge $5,000–$15,000/month for a human coach who can review 10 calls. Gunner reviews every call, automatically, for a fraction of that." The anchor is $5,000+. Now $197/month feels like a steal.

**B. Cost-of-problem anchoring** — Lead managers at NAH generate $8,000–$25,000/deal. If poor call performance costs even one deal per quarter, that's a $25,000 loss. An objection to a $197/month tool is irrational on its face once this anchor is set.

**C. Internal price anchoring** — On a three-tier pricing page, the premium tier anchors perception. The middle tier — the one you actually want people to buy — looks reasonable against the premium and generous against the starter. This is the **decoy effect** in action.

---

## 3. Decoy Pricing (Asymmetric Dominance)

The decoy effect works when you introduce an option that nobody will choose, but whose presence makes your preferred option look better.

**Classic Gunner example:**
- Starter: $79/month — 50 calls/month, no leaderboard
- Pro: $197/month — unlimited calls, full leaderboard, team analytics ← *target*
- Enterprise: $499/month — everything + white-glove onboarding

The Enterprise tier exists partly to make Pro look reasonable. Most buyers land on Pro. The decoy isn't a fake offer — it needs to be real — but it's designed to lose.

**The rule:** Design your pricing table so the "wrong" choice is obvious. Buyers shouldn't need to think hard. If they're confused, you lose them.

---

## 4. Charm Pricing & Left-Digit Effect

**Charm pricing** ($197 vs. $200) works because the brain reads left-to-right and anchors on the leftmost digit. $197 registers as "one hundred something" before the full number processes. $200 registers as "two hundred."

This effect is well-documented but often misapplied. Rules:
- Works best at lower price points (under $500/month)
- Loses effectiveness at enterprise pricing — $9,997 vs. $10,000 is noise
- For NAH: offer prices should be round numbers ($45,000, not $44,997) — roundness signals confidence, not gimmickry
- For Gunner: monthly subscriptions benefit from charm pricing; annual contracts less so

## 5. The Value Equation — Hormozi's Framework

Alex Hormozi's value equation from *$100M Offers* is the most useful pricing framework for operators:

```
Value = (Dream Outcome × Perceived Likelihood of Achievement)
        ÷ (Time Delay × Effort & Sacrifice)
```

To increase perceived value — and therefore justify higher prices — you either:
1. **Increase the dream outcome** (bigger results, more meaningful transformation)
2. **Increase perceived likelihood** (proof, case studies, guarantees, track record)
3. **Decrease time delay** (faster results — "see improvement in your first week")
4. **Decrease effort/sacrifice** (remove friction — "no setup, no training, works automatically")

**Gunner application:**
- Dream Outcome: Your team closes more deals, you stop losing money to poor call execution, you build a coaching culture without spending 10 hours a week reviewing calls.
- Likelihood: Show actual call grades from real wholesale teams. One real before/after is worth 100 feature bullets.
- Time: "First graded call appears within 60 seconds of the call ending."
- Effort: "Connects to GHL in 5 minutes. No training required."

**NAH seller application (acquiring distressed properties):**
- Dream Outcome: Cash, speed, no repairs, no agents, no stress.
- Likelihood: "We've closed 200+ deals in Nashville. Here's one we closed in 11 days."
- Time: "Close in as little as 7 days."
- Effort: "We handle everything. You just show up to closing."

The formula explains why lowering price is almost always the wrong response to an objection. A price objection means the denominator (time/effort) feels too high or the numerator (outcome/likelihood) feels too low. Fix the equation; don't just drop the number.

## 6. Fear-Based vs. Aspiration-Based Pricing Framing

Two emotional levers drive purchase decisions:

**Fear-based framing** — "What are you losing by not buying?"
- Loss aversion is 2× more powerful than equivalent gain (Kahneman/Tversky)
- Works when the pain is immediate and concrete
- "Every week you don't have Gunner, you're flying blind on your team's calls. That's deals slipping through without you knowing why."
- "Every day a motivated seller sits on your desk without an offer is a day they might call someone else."

**Aspiration-based framing** — "Who do you become when you buy?"
- Works better for identity-driven purchases
- Works better in initial marketing (attract) vs. closing (convert)
- "The teams using Gunner are building the first data-driven wholesale operations in the country."
- "Imagine knowing exactly which of your LMs is improving, which is stuck, and exactly what to say to fix it."

**The rule:** Use aspiration to open, fear to close. Fear in marketing feels manipulative and repels prospects. Fear in a sales conversation — when a prospect has already expressed interest — is a logical reframe, not manipulation.

## 7. Pricing SaaS (Gunner) vs. Pricing a Service (NAH)

### SaaS Pricing Principles for Gunner

**Value metric alignment** — The ideal SaaS pricing variable scales with the value the customer gets. Bad: per-seat pricing for a tool that graders calls automatically (seat count doesn't correlate with value). Better: per call reviewed, or flat monthly with call caps that scale by tier.

**Annual vs. monthly** — Annual plans reduce churn dramatically. A customer who paid $2,364 upfront has much higher psychological commitment than one paying $197/month. Offer 2 months free for annual. At Gunner's stage, push annual hard.

**The magic price zones for B2B SaaS:**
- Sub-$100/month: no-brainer, self-serve, low friction
- $100–$500/month: decision-maker can approve without finance; still self-serve possible
- $500–$2,000/month: VP-level approval; needs a champion; human touchpoint required
- $2,000+/month: procurement gets involved; sales cycle explodes

Gunner should stay in the $100–$500/month range until there's a clear enterprise motion.

**Price-to-value gap** — If you're solving a problem worth $50,000/year and charging $2,400/year, there's a massive gap. Narrow it over time by raising prices as you accumulate proof. The current Gunner pricing (if under $500/month) is almost certainly underpriced relative to the value delivered by even one recovered deal.

### Wholesale Deal Pricing (NAH)

**The MAO formula** — Maximum Allowable Offer = (ARV × 70%) – Repairs. This is not a psychological framework; it's a hard ceiling. Pricing psychology for NAH operates *around* the MAO, not above it.

**The seller psychology reframe** — Sellers often anchor to Zillow or to what they paid or to what their neighbor got. Your job is to re-anchor them to their actual problem: carrying costs, time, uncertainty, repair stress. The question isn't "is $45,000 a good price?" — it's "what is the cost of NOT taking $45,000 right now?"

**Speed as a price substitute** — You can often make a lower offer more attractive by dramatically compressing time. A motivated seller will sometimes take $10,000 less to close in 7 days versus 60. Time is part of the value equation, not just price.

## 8. Price Objection = Value Communication Failure

The single most important reframe in pricing: **a price objection is never really about the price.**

When a prospect says "that's too expensive," they are saying one or more of:
1. "I don't believe this will work for me." (Likelihood problem)
2. "I don't believe the outcome is worth that much." (Dream outcome not vivid enough)
3. "I can't afford it right now." (Timing/cash problem — real, but rare)
4. "I'm not the decision-maker." (Wrong person — not a price problem at all)
5. "I don't trust you yet." (Relationship/credibility problem)

**The right response to a price objection is a value question, not a discount:**
- "What would make you feel confident this would pay for itself?"
- "What's the cost to you if call quality stays where it is for another 6 months?"
- "What would change for your team if they improved close rate by even 10%?"

Only after you've exhausted value reframing should you consider any price flexibility — and then only in the form of payment structure (monthly vs. upfront, extended trial), not a straight discount, which damages perceived value permanently.

## 9. When to Raise vs. Lower Prices

### Raise prices when:
- Churn is low and customers are staying (demand inelastic — you're underpriced)
- Prospects rarely push back on price (you're probably leaving money on the table)
- Your cost to acquire a customer (CAC) is high relative to contract value
- You're attracting low-quality customers who complain a lot and pay little
- You've added significant value (new features, proven outcomes, case studies)

### Lower prices when:
- Win rate is under 20% and sales cycle feedback consistently cites price
- You're targeting a new segment with lower willingness to pay
- You need volume to build the data/social proof flywheel (early stage)
- A competitive threat has materially changed the market

**For Gunner at current stage:** Don't lower prices. Add proof. Add guarantee. Add onboarding support. Raise prices as you accumulate case studies. The first 20 customers should not pay the same as customer 200 — but not because you lowered prices. Because you raised them.

---

## 10. The Psychology of Guarantees

A strong guarantee doesn't just reduce risk — it signals confidence and shifts the psychological burden.

**The inversion principle:** A bold guarantee makes your price *feel* lower, even if the dollar amount is identical. "$197/month, cancel anytime, and if you don't see measurable improvement in 30 days, we'll refund your first month" feels different than "$197/month" alone, even though the financial exposure is the same.

**For Gunner:** "If your team's call scores don't improve in 60 days, we'll work with you for free until they do." This is low-risk to execute (teams that engage with the tool do improve) and high-value in the sales conversation.

---

## Summary: The Pricing Decision Tree

1. What is the cost of the problem we solve? → This is your price ceiling.
2. What does the buyer believe about that cost? → Close the gap with anchoring.
3. Does the buyer believe we'll solve it? → Add proof, guarantee, case studies.
4. Does the buyer believe it's worth the effort? → Reduce friction, compress onboarding.
5. Is the dollar amount the actual barrier? → Almost never. Fix the equation, not the price.
6. Is there a structural move (annual, payment plan, bundling) that changes the math? → Use it before discounting.

Price is a story. Tell it deliberately.
