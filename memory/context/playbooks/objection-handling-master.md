# Objection Handling Master Reference

This document is the definitive objection-handling reference for NAH's acquisition and lead management team. Cross-referenced with Todd Toback, Steve Trang, and field-tested patterns from wholesale real estate practice. Use this for call coaching, role-playing, and live call support.

---

## The Core Framework: Cushion → Reverse → Truth-Tell

Every objection response follows this three-part structure:

**1. Cushion** — Acknowledge without agreeing. Never fight the objection directly. The seller needs to feel heard before they'll hear you.

**2. Reverse** — Ask a clarifying question that turns the objection back into a conversation. The goal isn't to answer the objection; it's to understand what's underneath it.

**3. Truth-Tell** — Share a genuine, direct observation that reframes the situation. Not a pitch. A mirror.

## The 5 Core Objections

### Objection #1: "Your price is too low"

**What they mean:** *"I don't feel like you value my property."* Often signals an emotional attachment more than actual financial need.

**The Mistake:** Immediately justifying the number with comps. This creates a data war you can't win because their number is emotional, not logical.

**Cushion → Reverse → Truth-Tell:**

*Cushion:* "I completely understand — every seller wants to maximize what they get for their property, and that makes total sense."

*Reverse:* "Help me understand — when you say the price is too low, are you saying it doesn't meet a specific number you need, or does it just feel lower than you expected?"

*Truth-Tell (if they have a specific number):* "Okay, so you need $X to make this work. I respect that. Let me be straight with you — here's the math on our side: [ARV, repairs, margin]. If I could get to $X and still make this work, I would. The gap between what we need and what you need is $Y. What do you want to do with that information?"

**Steve Trang's Cost-of-Inaction Technique:**
After establishing their number, calculate what inaction costs them:
*"If you don't sell today and you're carrying this property for another 6 months — property taxes, insurance, maybe repairs — that's roughly $X per month. So waiting 6 months costs you $Y in real dollars. Does it make sense to wait?"*

This reframes the decision from "accept a low offer" to "how much is waiting worth to you?"

**Todd Toback's Equity Anchor:**
*"What's the minimum you'd take? Not what you want — the absolute minimum where you'd feel okay walking away."*
The minimum is almost always lower than the asking price. Once they say it, you work from there.

**Script fragment:**
> "I hear you. A lot of sellers feel that way at first. Can I ask — is the number the only thing keeping you from moving forward, or is there something else going on here?"

### Objection #2: "I need more time" / "I'm not ready yet"

**What they mean:** Usually one of three things: (a) they're emotionally not ready to let go, (b) they have an unresolved problem (probate, POA, family disagreement), or (c) they're shopping around.

**Diagnosis question first:** *"When you say you need more time — what specifically needs to happen before you'd feel ready?"*

This one question separates the emotional from the logistical.

**If it's emotional:**
*"That totally makes sense. This is your home and that's real. How long have you owned this property?"* → Let them talk. The story is the objection. Listen for the shift when they run out of reasons to hold on.

**If it's logistical (probate, POA, family):**
Get specific:
- Probate: "What's the status of the probate? Do you have an attorney? When does the court date happen?"
- Family disagreement: "Who else needs to be part of this decision? Could we get them on a call together?"
- POA: "Do you have power of attorney already notarized? We work with attorneys who can help move that quickly if needed."

**If they're shopping around (stealth signal):**
*"Fair enough. While you're thinking, are you talking to other buyers?"*
If yes: *"That's smart — you should compare. What would you need to see to feel confident we're the right buyer?"*

**The 24-Hour Anchor (Toback):**
*"I understand you need to think. Here's what I've seen — the sellers who wait often end up in the same spot 60 days later but with fewer options. I'm not trying to pressure you. Is there anything I can answer today that would help you feel more certain?"*

### Objection #3: "I have other offers" / "Someone else is offering more"

**What they mean:** Sometimes true. Often a negotiating tactic.

**Never:** Immediately match or undercut. This signals panic.

**The Qualification Step:**
*"That's great — are you already under contract with them, or is it still in conversation?"*
- If under contract: move to follow-up bucket, calendar check-in for 30 days
- If not under contract: *"So you're still deciding. What's keeping you from signing with them today?"*

**The Certainty Wedge:**
Most competing offers in wholesale are either not firm, not cash, or have conditions. Ask:
*"Is their offer fully cash, no inspection, close in 21 days like ours? Or does it have conditions?"*

Sellers often haven't asked this. You're not attacking the competition — you're introducing the concept of offer certainty.

**Todd Toback's Stealth Mismatch:**
Rather than attacking the other offer directly, create subtle doubt:
*"I'm sure they're a great buyer. The one thing I'd want you to verify is their proof of funds and their typical close timeline. We've had sellers choose a higher offer and then spend 60 days waiting to find out the buyer couldn't close. I'd hate that for you."*

This plants legitimate due diligence without discrediting you.

### Objection #4: "I don't know you / Why should I trust you?"

**What they mean:** *"You're a stranger and this is my biggest financial asset."* Totally valid.

**The Mistake:** Launching into credentials and reviews. This is defensive and signals insecurity.

**The Pattern:**
*"That's a fair question, and honestly, I'd be suspicious of any buyer you hadn't worked with before. What would make you feel confident enough to at least hear the offer?"*

Let them define what trust means to them. Then deliver it.

**If they want testimonials/references:**
*"Absolutely — let me send you two or three sellers we've worked with in Nashville who've given us permission to share their experience. Can I text you that today?"*

**If they're skeptical of the whole wholesale process:**
*"I get it — it sounds too good to be true that we can close in 21 days with no repairs. The way it works is [brief, clear explanation of wholesale]. Does that make sense?"*

**Building rapport before credibility:**
Ask about the property's story first. People trust people who listen, not people who pitch. Ten minutes of genuine curiosity about why they're selling, what the house meant to them, what they're hoping to do next — that builds more trust than any testimonial.

---

### Objection #5: "I need to think about it" / "Let me talk to my [spouse/kids/attorney]"

**The trap:** Ending the call. Once they hang up with "I'll think about it," you're at the mercy of their mental environment — and competitors.

**First move — identify the real objection:**
*"Of course, take all the time you need. Before I let you go — is the 'thinking about it' about the price, the process, or something else I haven't addressed?"*

Most sellers have a real concern underneath. "Think about it" is a polite exit, not an objection. Get to the real one.

**If they name it:** Address it now. The phone call is the best time.

**If they genuinely need to consult family:**
*"That makes total sense — I want everyone on the same page. Can we schedule a time when we can all be on a call together? I can answer questions for everyone at once. What works this week?"*

Offer to do the family call. This removes the "I'll pass along the information" step where nuance gets lost and your pitch gets mangled.

**The Urgency Frame (without pressure):**
*"I'll respect whatever decision you make. I'll mention that our acquisition pipeline fills up and I can only hold off on other offers for 48 hours — not to pressure you, just so you can factor that in."*

## Personality-Adaptive Responses

### Analytical Seller (detail-oriented, asks lots of questions, slow decision maker)
- **Signals:** Asks about comps, ARV, your process, wants to see numbers
- **Approach:** Give them data. Don't rush. Send the math in writing. Acknowledge their research.
- **Mistake to avoid:** Emotional appeals ("this is a great opportunity!") — they need logic, not excitement
- **On price objection:** Go deep on the repair cost breakdown, your fee structure, comparable sales. Respect their intellect.

### Expressive Seller (emotional, story-teller, relationship-focused)
- **Signals:** Talks about memories in the house, family, wants to know about you personally
- **Approach:** Stay with the story. Ask about the house's history. Connect before converting.
- **Mistake to avoid:** Rushing to numbers too fast — they'll shut down
- **On trust objection:** Share a brief personal story about a past seller or your team's mission

### Driver Seller (direct, no-nonsense, wants the bottom line)
- **Signals:** "Just give me a number." Interrupts. Hates pleasantries.
- **Approach:** Bottom line first. Skip the rapport-building preamble. Respect their time.
- **Mistake to avoid:** Over-explaining, hedging, being wishy-washy on numbers
- **On competing offer objection:** Match their directness: "Fair enough. Here's our number, here's our timeline, here's how we're different. What do you want to do?"

### Amiable Seller (agreeable, avoidant of conflict, hard to pin down)
- **Signals:** "Sounds good" to everything but never commits. Says "I'll think about it" repeatedly.
- **Approach:** Create gentle structure. Give them limited choices rather than open questions.
- **Mistake to avoid:** Leaving decisions too open-ended — they'll float indefinitely
- **On "think about it":** "I understand. Would it be easier to make a decision by Friday, or would Monday work better?" → forcing a timeline without confrontation

## Quick-Reference Script Fragments

### Opening a stalled lead (cold re-engage):
> "Hey [name], this is [name] from New Again Houses — we spoke a few months back about your property on [street]. Just wanted to check in. Are you still considering any options for the property, or has the situation changed?"

### After they say "too low":
> "I hear you. Let's figure out if there's a path here together. What number would make this feel right to you?"

### When they go silent / seem disengaged:
> "I feel like I might have lost you there — am I off base, or is there something I haven't addressed?"

### Closing a conversation with an undecided seller:
> "I respect whatever you decide. Before I let you go — what's the one thing that would need to be true for you to say yes to this?"

## Anti-Patterns to Eliminate

- ❌ **Arguing with the objection.** ("No, our price IS fair because...") — Creates resistance.
- ❌ **Folding immediately.** ("Okay, we can go higher.") — Signals weakness, leads to endless renegotiation.
- ❌ **Over-explaining.** Giving a 3-minute monologue in response to a one-line objection.
- ❌ **Treating "think about it" as a yes.** It's a soft no until re-engaged.
- ❌ **Skipping to price before understanding the seller's situation.** You can't handle what you don't understand.
